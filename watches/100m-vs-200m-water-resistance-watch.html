Water resistance changes as seals age

Water resistance is not permanent. Seals age, crowns can be left unscrewed, and impact or service can affect the case. A watch that was water resistant when new may not remain so indefinitely. Follow the manufacturer’s recommended pressure testing and service interval.

What the depth marking can—and cannot—tell you

A depth marking does not identify every activity the manufacturer allows. Read the model’s instructions for swimming, diving, crown position and button use; follow the stated service guidance. The condition of the seals matters, and a familiar rating cannot establish the condition of a used or modified watch.

A diver’s watch needs more than a number

A 100m marking often suits more everyday water exposure than a basic splash rating, but model instructions control. Do not infer scuba suitability from the number alone. ISO 6425 applies to diver watches designed for at least 100 metres and includes additional requirements and test methods.

Crown position matters before entering the water

A screw-down crown helps protect some cases only when it is correctly secured. Never operate the crown or chronograph pushers underwater unless the manual explicitly allows it. Rinse a watch after salt or chlorinated water if the maker recommends it, and dry it carefully.

Keep hot showers out of the experiment

Heat, soap, solvents and rapid temperature changes can challenge seals. Hot showers and saunas are poor tests of water resistance because heat and detergents may affect materials. If condensation appears under the crystal, seek prompt service rather than leaving the watch to dry on a shelf.

What extra rated margin buys you

The 200m label provides more rated margin than 100m within a model’s specified use, but it does not mean twice the practical safety or twice the service interval. Case construction, seals, age and manufacturer testing matter. A certified diver’s watch has requirements beyond a depth marking.

When to ask for a pressure test

Before swimming or diving, check the exact reference, instruction manual and recent pressure-test status. If use is critical, ask an authorized service provider to test it. A watch listing, bezel or marketing photo cannot establish current seal condition.

Frequently asked questions

Can I swim with a 100m watch?

Check the exact maker guidance and condition; water resistance can decline as seals age.

Does 200m mean safe to 200 metres?

No. The marking is based on test conditions and should not be read as a real-world depth guarantee.

Is a diver-style watch ISO certified?

Not automatically. Look for explicit ISO 6425 conformity from the manufacturer.
