Seiko 4R36 Accuracy: How to Measure a Watch That Runs Fast or Slow

A repeatable seven-day check tells you more than one alarming reading.

What is the official 4R36 accuracy range?

Seiko lists a factory-adjusted daily rate of −35 to +45 seconds for the 4R36 in its current shared instruction manual, with the stated wrist-wearing temperature condition of 5–35°C. Those boundaries are useful context, not a promise that every measurement in every position will fit inside them. Check the manual for your exact watch reference before comparing results. Source: Seiko specifications.

The number on a seller's page may describe a different movement or the seller's own regulation standard. Do not silently replace a 4R36 specification with an NH36 number. If the caseback and paperwork disagree, record the full watch reference and ask the seller to identify the fitted movement. You do not need to open the watch to begin this conversation.

A practical question is whether the watch behaves consistently enough for your routine. A predictable gain that you correct occasionally is different from a watch that alternates between moderate gain and unexplained large losses. The second pattern gives a technician different information, even if both owners describe their watches as inaccurate.

Total offset and daily rate are different measurements

Offset is how far the displayed time differs from the reference right now. Use a plus sign when the watch is ahead and a minus sign when it is behind. Daily rate is how much that offset changed over a measured interval, expressed per 24 hours.

For example, a watch that was 12 seconds ahead yesterday and is 25 seconds ahead today gained 13 seconds over that day. It did not gain 25 seconds in the latest interval. If it starts 20 seconds behind and finishes 5 seconds behind, it gained 15 seconds even though it still shows an earlier time than the reference.These worked timing logs illustrate how to calculate daily positional drift.

Daily rate = (ending offset − starting offset) × 24 ÷ elapsed hours

Recording the signs prevents a common error: calling every negative reading a loss during the interval. If you missed your usual checking time, write the actual elapsed hours. A 36-hour change should not be treated as a 24-hour change. The calculator below handles that difference.

Build a useful seven-day record

Pick a time you can keep, such as after breakfast. Use one stable reference throughout; switching between devices introduces another possible difference. Make the seconds display easy to compare. Read in a calm moment rather than trying to estimate while walking or while the reference page is still loading.

  1. Start with a running watch. Follow the model's winding and crown instructions. Note your preparation instead of assuming that a moving seconds hand proves a full reserve.
  2. Record a baseline. You may synchronize once, or simply record the starting offset. Neither approach requires the first reading to be zero.
  3. Check at the same time daily. Write the signed offset and the real observation time. Leave the watch running between checks.
  4. Describe the routine. Note approximate wear time, whether the day was mostly desk work, and the overnight resting position.
  5. Record interruptions. If you reset the time, stop the seconds hand, or the watch stops, end that interval. Begin a new baseline rather than blending the readings.

Seiko recommends judging mechanical accuracy across roughly a week because wearing conditions and winding state affect the result. Its accuracy guidance also explains positional and temperature effects. Source: Seiko mechanical accuracy guidance. Read the pattern before trying to fix it

Compare each day's change, then calculate the overall change between the first and last valid readings. Seven dated readings usually contain six intervals; if you want seven complete 24-hour intervals, include the starting baseline plus seven follow-up readings. Count the elapsed time instead of dividing automatically by the number of rows.

Change one variable at a time if you want to explore your routine. You could keep wear time similar and compare two overnight positions across separate periods. Do not assume dial-up will always slow a watch or crown-down will always speed it up. The useful result is what your watch repeatedly does, not what another owner's watch does.

When to stop experimenting and contact service

If the watch repeatedly performs poorly relative to its applicable guidance under ordinary, documented use, ask the retailer or service center to assess it. A sudden major change, stopping despite appropriate winding, moisture under the crystal, or an unusual crown feel is more informative than a single daily average.

Seiko notes that strong magnetic exposure can affect rate and recommends consulting the retailer when demagnetizing or repair is needed. That does not make magnetism the explanation for every fast watch. A timing log alone cannot identify a magnetized hairspring, damaged component or lubrication problem. Source: Seiko accuracy guidance.

Send a concise message: watch reference, approximate purchase date, normal routine, start and end offsets, elapsed time, and any recent change. Ask whether the inspection includes rate assessment, power-reserve concerns and the relevant warranty procedure. Avoid opening the case or moving a regulator just to see what happens; doing so changes the problem you are trying to document.

What should you buy, if anything?

You can do this first check with the watch, a dependable time reference and a notebook. A timing machine is not necessary to learn whether the displayed time drifts consistently. Its short readings answer a different question and require suitable setup and interpretation.

If your actual need is to pick up a watch after several unworn days without resetting it, think about the movement type before spending on accessories. Our automatic versus solar guide explains that routine. If the calendar is wrong but the time remains steady, use the day and date setting tutorial. Buying another watch is not the first step in either diagnosis.

Step-by-Step Diagnosis: Separating Normal Positional Drift from Magnetization

Although Seiko's user manual prints a wide factory specification of -35 to +45 seconds per day for the Caliber 4R36 (and 4R35 / 4R34), a healthy, unmagnetized 4R36 worn 10 hours a day typically settles between -5 and +15 seconds per day once broken in. How it drifts tells you immediately what is happening inside the balance assembly:

Observed Daily Drift PatternMost Likely Mechanical CauseRecommended Fix (Zero-Cost to Watchmaker)
Steady +5 to +15 sec/day every dayNormal factory regulation (slightly biased positive so you aren't late)Rest watch Crown-Down (9 o'clock up) overnight to shed 2–4 seconds
Steady -8 to -18 sec/day every dayNormal regulation on the slow side or low mainspring amplitudeWind 20 crown turns before bed and rest Dial-Up flat overnight
Suddenly running +45 to +300 sec/day fastMagnetized Spron 110 hairspring (coils sticking together, shortening swing)Pass through a $12 blue demagnetizer tool or visit a watchmaker (2-min fix)
Erratic (+20s one day, -25s the next)Low power reserve from sedentary desk wear or hairspring caught on stud after dropTest after 40 full hand-winding turns; if still erratic, inspect balance hairspring
Gradually slowing down after 6–10 yearsDried lubricants in escapement and pallet fork jewelsStandard movement service or ~$45 TMI NH36A drop-in replacement

Before touching a caseback opener, check for magnetization—the culprit behind 80% of sudden fast-running Seiko 5 watches. Modern desks are minefields of neodymium magnets: MacBook palm rests, iPad Smart Covers, MagSafe chargers, wireless earbud cases, and magnetic bag clasps all generate localized fields strong enough to magnetize a steel hairspring when you rest your watch next to them. When two coils of the hairspring stick together magnetically, the effective spring length shortens and the balance wheel oscillates dangerously fast, gaining 1 to 10 minutes a day. A $12 electronic demagnetizer (or a free 30-second pass at your local watch repair counter) restores normal accuracy immediately without opening the watch.

Questions readers ask

Is +20 seconds a day automatically a fault?

Not by itself. Compare a consistent multi-day record with the exact movement and watch guidance, including the stated conditions. A sudden change from earlier behavior is also worth documenting.

Should I reset the watch every morning?

Not during the same measurement run. Resetting removes the continuous offset history. If you reset it, mark the interruption and start a new interval.

Which overnight resting position slows down or speeds up a Seiko 4R36?

Because gravity pulls differently on the balance wheel in vertical vs horizontal orientations, resting a Seiko 4R36 flat Dial-Up usually yields its fastest rate (+2 to +5 seconds over 8 hours), whereas resting it vertically Crown-Down (on its 9 o'clock flank) or Crown-Up typically slows the rate slightly by 2 to 6 seconds overnight.

Should I regulate the Etachron +/- lever on a Seiko 4R36 myself?

Only if you have a timegrapher (or smartphone acoustic timegrapher microphone) and proper caseback tools. On the 4R36 balance cock, the outer key-slotted pointer adjusts daily rate (+/-) while the inner stud carrier adjusts beat error—accidentally slipping a screwdriver into the hairspring below destroys the balance assembly instantly.

Complete Buyer's Inspection, Sizing & Long-Term Ownership Protocol

When your new watch arrives—whether you ordered from an authorized dealer, a Japanese domestic market (JDM) exporter, or Amazon fulfillment—running a disciplined 10-minute inspection before peeling the bracelet protective plastics or sizing links protects your return window and guarantees you got a clean factory sample. Even respected high-volume manufacturers like Seiko, Citizen, Orient, and Casio produce occasional assembly tolerances that are effortless to exchange on day one while the stickers are intact, but frustrating to discover two weeks later after scratching the clasp at your desk.

1. Optical Alignment, Hand Clearance, and Bezel Indexing Check

Start under bright natural window light or a desk lamp. Advance the hour and minute hands slowly through a full 24-hour cycle using the crown in its outer time-setting position. Watch three critical clearances as the hands pass 12:00: first, verify that the hour hand points dead-center at the 12 o'clock marker when the minute hand crosses zero (hand-stack indexing error should be less than half a minute-track width). Second, view the dial from a low side angle to confirm that the hour, minute, and seconds hands run strictly parallel to the dial plane without tilting upward toward the crystal or rubbing against applied hour indices. Third, watch the date and day wheels between 11:45 PM and 12:05 AM (or up to 2:45 AM for the secondary bilingual day wheel click on Seiko 4R36/NH36 calibers) to confirm crisp centering inside the dial window frame.

If your watch has a unidirectional rotating dive bezel (60-click or 120-click), rotate it one full revolution counter-clockwise. The 12 o'clock luminous pip should land cleanly over the 12 o'clock dial marker—or within half a click of play—without vertical mushiness, and the chapter ring minute ticks should align cleanly with the dial indices at 12, 3, 6, and 9 o'clock.

2. Sizing Your Bracelet & Choosing Seasonal Straps for All-Day Comfort

Sizing a stainless steel bracelet properly comes down to two rules beyond just removing enough links to match your wrist circumference: centering the clasp under your wrist and leaving room for afternoon thermal expansion. Because the outside of your forearm (the 12 o'clock side of the watch) has a steeper radius than the inside of your wrist (the 6 o'clock side), you should always keep one or two fewer removable links on the 6 o'clock side than on the 12 o'clock side. That pulls the watch head slightly toward you so the dial faces your eyes naturally instead of rolling away toward your pinky bone, and centers the folding clasp blade squarely across the flat underside of your wrist.

When setting the micro-adjustment pin holes on the clasp, size the bracelet so you can slide the tip of your index finger snugly between the clasp and your wrist in a cool morning room. When you step outside into summer humidity or walk briskly for twenty minutes, your wrist expands by roughly 3mm to 5mm—having that half-link of breathing room prevents crown bite and sweat buildup without letting the watch spin around your wrist.

3. Magnetism, Gasket Care, and 10-Year Service Intervals

For mechanical watches, two simple habits prevent 90% of premature watchmaker visits: keep the watch at least six inches away from tablet folio magnets, magnetic laptop latches, and wireless charging pucks on your nightstand; and never operate the quickset date mechanism when the hands read between 9:00 PM and 3:00 AM. For solar and quartz watches, wipe sweat and sunscreen off the caseback and bezel crevices with a damp microfiber cloth once a week, and keep solar dials exposed to natural room light rather than buried inside a dark closet drawer. Every two to three years—or before any ocean vacation—have a local watchmaker run a quick dry pressure test on the case gaskets so your water resistance rating stays as reliable in year eight as it was on day one.
