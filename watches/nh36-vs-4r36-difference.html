NH36 versus 4R36: begin with the name on the paperwork

You have found two similar watches. One listing says NH36; the other says 4R36. Both have a moving seconds hand and a day-date window, and the seller describes each as a Seiko movement. Is one automatically better? The useful answer begins with which organisation documents the movement and which company is responsible for the finished watch.

TMI publishes technical information for the NH36, used in watches assembled by different brands and builders. Seiko publishes consumer instructions for the 4R36 in its own watches. They have closely related functions and specifications, but those names should not erase the differences between the products being sold. A movement's origin doesn't identify the maker of every case, dial, bracelet or finished assembly around it.

The official photograph on this page shows a real Seiko SRPD55, whose product page identifies its 4R36 movement. It is a finished-watch example, not a photograph of an NH36, a dismantled calibre or an aftermarket conversion. Technical diagrams below are explanatory drawings. They do not document a movement we opened or tested.

For a purchase, write down the complete watch reference, stated calibre, assembler or brand, seller and support route. Those five pieces of information prevent a surprising amount of confusion. If a custom watch has an NH36, evaluate it as the builder's watch. A familiar calibre can be a useful feature without turning that watch into a factory Seiko model.

The functions you can reasonably compare

The documented comparison centres on automatic winding, manual winding, stop-seconds and the day-date calendar. Both movements operate at 21,600 vibrations per hour and use twenty-four jewels in the cited specifications. These facts describe functions and construction. They do not establish the cosmetic finishing, regulation, assembly quality or condition of a watch offered by a particular seller.

At this frequency, there are six beats per second. That can help explain the visible movement of the seconds hand, but it isn't a measurement of accuracy. A faster-beating movement isn't automatically better adjusted, and two watches with the same frequency can keep different rates. Decide whether the hand's appearance matters to you without using it as a shortcut for quality.

Stop-seconds makes it easier to synchronize the displayed time during setting. Manual winding offers a way to prepare a stopped watch according to its instructions. Neither feature removes the need to learn crown positions or the calendar's setting restrictions. On a finished watch, a screw-down crown adds another operating step before those positions become available.

The published reserve figures are also similar in scale: Seiko describes approximately forty-one hours, while the TMI guide specifies more than forty-one hours under its stated full-wind test condition. Similar numbers don't mean every loosely worn watch will run that long after you put it down. Energy preparation, use and the precise measurement conditions still matter.

Why the accuracy ranges look different

The cited Seiko consumer manual lists a factory-adjusted daily rate of minus thirty-five to plus forty-five seconds, with a wrist-wearing temperature condition of five to thirty-five degrees Celsius. TMI's NH36 technical guide gives a static range of minus twenty to plus forty seconds per day under its stated measurement procedure. Those are different specifications presented for different contexts.

It is tempting to compare the narrower-looking range and conclude that every NH36 is more accurate than every 4R36. That conclusion isn't supported. The conditions and purpose of the figures differ. You would need comparable measurements of identified samples under a disclosed method to make a tested comparison, and even then the sample size would limit the conclusion.

Think of a specification as a question to ask correctly. Which document applies to this movement? How was the result measured? What does the watch brand promise for the finished watch? A builder may state a separate adjustment target, but it should come with conditions and a support policy. A bare “regulated” label doesn't tell you how or when the work was checked.

This is also why an online comment saying “mine runs at plus two” is interesting rather than decisive. It may describe a well-adjusted example, a short observation or a particular wearing routine. It is not a factory standard for everything with the same calibre name. Use such comments to learn what to measure, rather than to create a guarantee for your future watch.

A timegrapher reading needs its conditions attached

TMI specifies measurement details including winding state, timing after full winding and positions. Its NH36 technical guide gives a fifty-three-degree lift angle for the stated procedure. If a seller supplies a timegrapher photograph, ask whether the setup matches the movement documentation. An attractive trace without the relevant settings is incomplete evidence.

Ask when the measurement was taken and what position was used. A single dial-up reading doesn't tell you every wearing condition. Ask whether the photograph belongs to the actual movement or watch being sold, rather than a representative batch. If the seller is advertising an adjustment standard, the reply should explain what that standard means for your purchase and any later support.

Don't treat the machine's rate, amplitude and beat-error fields as interchangeable scores. They describe different observations under the test setup. This guide does not supply diagnostic thresholds or instruct you to adjust the regulator based on one picture. A qualified technician can interpret the complete evidence and decide what checks are appropriate for the particular watch.

A phone app is another measurement tool with its own limitations. A conventional comparison with a reference clock establishes an owner's observed offset. An app claiming to analyse ticking needs a disclosed method and validated setup before its output can be treated like a professional machine reading. Record what tool was used rather than calling every estimate a laboratory result.

Make an owner log before deciding that something is wrong

Start with a running watch prepared according to the finished watch's instructions. Choose one reference clock and a checking time you can repeat. Record the signed difference: a plus sign if the watch is ahead, a minus sign if it is behind. Then leave it running between observations. Synchronizing repeatedly destroys the continuous record you are trying to understand.

The daily change is the difference between two offsets, adjusted for elapsed time. If yesterday's offset was minus twenty seconds and today's is minus five after twenty-four hours, the watch gained fifteen seconds during that interval. It is still behind the reference, but it did not lose another five seconds. This is a teaching example rather than data from a sample.

Note wearing time, rest position, winding and any interruption. If the watch stops or you reset it, mark that point and begin a new interval. Avoid inventing missing readings or dividing by seven simply because your sheet has seven rows. Count the actual time between the first and last valid observations.

The linked seven-day accuracy tutorial includes a calculator and downloadable log. Here, the purpose is comparison discipline: an owner observation, a seller's timegrapher record and a manufacturer's specification answer different questions. Keeping their methods visible helps you discuss a problem without pretending they are identical forms of evidence.

NH36 does not make every mod a factory Seiko

A custom watch can be a worthwhile purchase. You may like its case size, dial style or bracelet more than a factory model. The important question is who assembled it, what components were used and who will help if something is wrong. Don't let a familiar movement name substitute for those answers.

Ask the builder to identify the movement, dial, case, crystal and crown, and describe what was checked after assembly. Ask whether the product is clearly presented as a custom build. Branding and a movement description alone should not be treated as authentication of the entire watch. Keep the invoice and builder's promises together.

Water resistance belongs to the completed assembly. It depends on the case, seals, crown and their condition; it is not a depth rating inherited from the movement. An aftermarket case description and a well-known calibre do not establish that the finished watch was pressure checked. If water use matters, ask for relevant documentation and qualified service guidance.

The same principle applies to future maintenance. A common movement family may make a technical conversation easier, but unusual case parts or a poorly documented build can still complicate repair. Ask about replacement crowns, stems, gaskets and crystals. A serviceable mechanism is only one part of a watch you hope to keep using.

Compatibility: use a parts checklist, not a nickname

The phrase “fits NH36” can describe only one aspect of a component. A successful build still needs compatible dial attachment, hand dimensions and clearance, calendar alignment, movement support, stem position and finished-case geometry. Confirm the exact versions and drawings with the parts suppliers. Similar calibre names are not a complete compatibility certificate.

Start with the calendar window. A date-only dial or an incorrectly positioned opening cannot be treated as a complete day-date match simply because the movement goes into the case. The display needs to align with the intended layout. Calendar language and disc version may also matter to the customer. Specify them before ordering instead of hoping they can be changed easily later.

Crown position deserves its own check. A case and dial arrangement can require a particular relationship between the stem and display. Ask the supplier to state the intended orientation and compatible movement version. Do not infer a guaranteed fit from a listing photograph where the assembled parts look similar. A drawing or written compatibility statement is much more useful.

Hand clearance and case support are equally important. Hands need to work together without interference and fit under the selected crystal, while the movement must be supported correctly inside the case. These are assembly questions, not cosmetic preferences. The checklist on this page collects the questions to ask; it does not certify a set of parts or provide a complete watchmaking procedure.

What to ask before buying a replacement movement

First establish the problem. A stopped watch could involve its power state, hands, crown or another component. Buying a movement because an online discussion mentions a common fault is a guess, and opening the case may affect seals or warranty. If the watch is covered, contact the responsible seller or service centre before modifying it.

If a professional has identified a movement replacement as appropriate, ask which exact version is needed. Include day and date configuration, disc language, stem arrangement and any required surrounding parts. Agree on what is included in the quote. A movement-only price and a completed, checked repair are different amounts for different work.

Keep replacement provenance and condition visible. New, used and previously adjusted movements are different products. Ask what supplier documentation comes with the item and whether returns cover an incompatible version. Don't assume a low-cost part sold with a familiar calibre name has the same support route as a factory watch purchased new.

After work, ask what checks were performed on the completed watch. Timekeeping, calendar function, hand clearance and the appropriate case checks are distinct tasks. The exact service scope should be documented. This information makes future repair easier and helps you understand what changed, without pretending that a movement swap automatically renews the whole watch.

Compare complete offers, not just calibres

Suppose a hypothetical custom NH36 watch and a factory 4R36 watch cost the same. The movement names alone do not decide between them. Compare fit, dial readability, bracelet, crystal, assembly disclosure, return conditions and service responsibility. The custom model might suit your taste; the factory model might offer a clearer local service route. Verify the actual offers.

If the custom watch costs less, write down what accounts for the difference before calling it better value. If the factory watch costs more, decide which benefits you would actually use. Paying for a support route can make sense, but a higher price isn't proof of superior timing. Keep preferences, documented features and measured results in separate columns.

Review seller claims using the same discipline. “Seiko movement,” “Japanese automatic” and “regulated” can all leave important questions unanswered. Ask for the specific calibre and finished-watch policy. A seller who clearly describes a custom build, publishes its checks and answers precise questions gives you more usable information than one relying on broad prestige language.

When regulation is—and is not—the next step

If a consistent owner log shows behavior you want assessed, send the record to the responsible service provider. Include the reference, calibre, wearing routine and whether the behavior changed suddenly. Ask them to explain the inspection and any proposed work. That is more useful than requesting a regulator adjustment based solely on a number copied from a forum.

Regulation is not a universal answer to every running problem. A technician may need to investigate other causes, and a single displayed rate does not establish what they are. This guide deliberately avoids instructing a beginner to open the case and move a lever. The watch is a complete assembly whose condition and coverage deserve consideration before work begins.

For a prospective purchase, request clear adjustment evidence if it is advertised, then judge it alongside the rest of the offer. For a watch you already enjoy, repeatable observations and a sensible service conversation may be all you need. Replacing a good watch with another using the same family of movement does not guarantee a different experience.

The practical answer to NH36 versus 4R36 is therefore a method rather than a universal winner: identify the product, compare documented functions, keep measurement conditions attached, and verify the finished watch's construction and support. Once those questions are answered, choose the watch that fits your life. A calibre name helps you ask better questions; it shouldn't be asked to answer all of them.

Questions readers ask

Does the NH36 published range prove it is always more accurate?

No. TMI static specifications and Seiko consumer wrist-wearing specifications have different conditions. A narrower-looking range is not a measured comparison of every finished watch.

Can I buy parts just because a listing says “NH36 compatible”?

Confirm the exact movement version, calendar layout, stem arrangement, dial, hands and case geometry with suppliers. A general label does not certify a complete assembly.
