Compare exact Seiko and Orient references

Seiko 5 is a collection, not a single specification. Its references can differ in case size, bezel, dial, movement and water resistance. The official 5 Sports collection and the watch caseback provide a better basis than an old review covering another variant.

What the crystal changes in daily ownership

Kamasu commonly refers to Orient’s diver-style line, but reference generations, markets and listings can differ. Verify the exact model code and manufacturer specifications, including movement, crystal, bracelet and water resistance, before treating two listings as equivalent.

Read the water rating with the instructions

A diver-style bezel is useful for timing a short interval, but appearance does not make a watch suitable for scuba. ISO 6425 sets requirements for diver watches designed for at least 100 metres and a secured timing system visible in darkness. A consumer “water resistant” marking is not the same as that certification.

Check case size and bracelet fit together

Case diameter can be misleading. Compare lug-to-lug, thickness and bracelet end links; a smaller diameter with long lugs can still dominate a narrow wrist. Try the watch on if possible, or buy through a seller with a clear return policy.

Movement specifications are a starting point

Movement comparisons should be specific. Seiko’s 4R36 instruction material describes operating and care details for that caliber, but not every Seiko 5 uses the same movement. Orient movements vary by model too. Compare hand-winding, hacking, reserve and service options on the exact references.

Include servicing in the purchase decision

A hardlex or mineral crystal may be easier to replace or less costly, while sapphire resists scratches better but can chip under impact. Check the exact material and bezel insert rather than assuming all watches in a family share it.

Which compromises suit your routine?

Choose a daily companion by comfort, legibility and maintenance expectations. The Kamasu may appeal if you want a more overt dive-watch look; Seiko 5 offers many dial and case styles. Neither is objectively better without a use case and exact model comparison.

Frequently asked questions

Which is more accurate?

Accuracy depends on the specific movement and regulation; neither brand family guarantees one fixed daily rate.

Can I swim with either?

Only follow the water-resistance guidance for the exact reference and condition of its seals.

Which fits smaller wrists?

Compare lug-to-lug and case thickness for the exact reference; names alone do not predict fit.

Is the Orient Kamasu crown really hard to unscrew and thread back in?

Because the Kamasu uses a compact ~5mm crown flanked by pointed crown guards, big fingers can find it slightly tight to grip. The trick is to push the crown gently toward the case while turning counter-clockwise one quarter-turn until you feel the thread 'drop' into the starting groove, then screw it clockwise smoothly with zero cross-threading risk.

Are Orient and Seiko owned by the same parent company?

Both brands belong to the broader Seiko Group in Japan, but they operate under separate corporate divisions: Seiko Watch Corporation produces Seiko and Grand Seiko, while Seiko Epson Corporation produces Orient, Orient Star, and Trume using Orient's own independent F6-series mechanical movements.

Head-to-Head Spec & Street Price Breakdown: Seiko 5 Sports SRPD vs Orient Kamasu

If you have $200 to $275 to spend on a Japanese automatic dive-style watch, the decision almost always comes down to two heavyweights: the Seiko 5 Sports SRPD series (such as the SRPD55, ~$205–$260) and the Orient Kamasu / Mako III (RA-AA0001B19B / RA-AA0004E19B, ~$215–$270). On paper, the Orient Kamasu wins the spec-sheet battle outright: for roughly the same street price, Orient gives you a flat scratch-resistant sapphire crystal (instead of Seiko's mineral Hardlex) and 200m water resistance with a screw-down crown (instead of Seiko's 100m push-pull crown).

Why do so many collectors still buy the Seiko 5 Sports SRPD anyway? Because wrist feel, crown ergonomics, and design personality matter just as much as a spec table. The SRPD's recessed 4 o'clock crown never digs into the back of your hand, its short 46.0 mm lug-to-lug length hugs smaller wrists effortlessly, its exhibition glass caseback lets you watch the 4R36 balance wheel tick, and its SKX007 heritage gives you hundreds of dial colors and aftermarket strap/mod options. Meanwhile, the Kamasu's small 4.5 mm screw-down crown at 3 o'clock can feel slightly stiff and finicky when threading it back in, even though its deep sunburst teal, burgundy, and navy dials punch way above $250.

Complete Buyer's Inspection, Sizing & Long-Term Ownership Protocol

When your new watch arrives—whether you ordered from an authorized dealer, a Japanese domestic market (JDM) exporter, or Amazon fulfillment—running a disciplined 10-minute inspection before peeling the bracelet protective plastics or sizing links protects your return window and guarantees you got a clean factory sample. Even respected high-volume manufacturers like Seiko, Citizen, Orient, and Casio produce occasional assembly tolerances that are effortless to exchange on day one while the stickers are intact, but frustrating to discover two weeks later after scratching the clasp at your desk.

1. Optical Alignment, Hand Clearance, and Bezel Indexing Check

Start under bright natural window light or a desk lamp. Advance the hour and minute hands slowly through a full 24-hour cycle using the crown in its outer time-setting position. Watch three critical clearances as the hands pass 12:00: first, verify that the hour hand points dead-center at the 12 o'clock marker when the minute hand crosses zero (hand-stack indexing error should be less than half a minute-track width). Second, view the dial from a low side angle to confirm that the hour, minute, and seconds hands run strictly parallel to the dial plane without tilting upward toward the crystal or rubbing against applied hour indices. Third, watch the date and day wheels between 11:45 PM and 12:05 AM (or up to 2:45 AM for the secondary bilingual day wheel click on Seiko 4R36/NH36 calibers) to confirm crisp centering inside the dial window frame.

If your watch has a unidirectional rotating dive bezel (60-click or 120-click), rotate it one full revolution counter-clockwise. The 12 o'clock luminous pip should land cleanly over the 12 o'clock dial marker—or within half a click of play—without vertical mushiness, and the chapter ring minute ticks should align cleanly with the dial indices at 12, 3, 6, and 9 o'clock.

2. Sizing Your Bracelet & Choosing Seasonal Straps for All-Day Comfort

Sizing a stainless steel bracelet properly comes down to two rules beyond just removing enough links to match your wrist circumference: centering the clasp under your wrist and leaving room for afternoon thermal expansion. Because the outside of your forearm (the 12 o'clock side of the watch) has a steeper radius than the inside of your wrist (the 6 o'clock side), you should always keep one or two fewer removable links on the 6 o'clock side than on the 12 o'clock side. That pulls the watch head slightly toward you so the dial faces your eyes naturally instead of rolling away toward your pinky bone, and centers the folding clasp blade squarely across the flat underside of your wrist.

When setting the micro-adjustment pin holes on the clasp, size the bracelet so you can slide the tip of your index finger snugly between the clasp and your wrist in a cool morning room. When you step outside into summer humidity or walk briskly for twenty minutes, your wrist expands by roughly 3mm to 5mm—having that half-link of breathing room prevents crown bite and sweat buildup without letting the watch spin around your wrist.

3. Magnetism, Gasket Care, and 10-Year Service Intervals

For mechanical watches, two simple habits prevent 90% of premature watchmaker visits: keep the watch at least six inches away from tablet folio magnets, magnetic laptop latches, and wireless charging pucks on your nightstand; and never operate the quickset date mechanism when the hands read between 9:00 PM and 3:00 AM. For solar and quartz watches, wipe sweat and sunscreen off the caseback and bezel crevices with a damp microfiber cloth once a week, and keep solar dials exposed to natural room light rather than buried inside a dark closet drawer. Every two to three years—or before any ocean vacation—have a local watchmaker run a quick dry pressure test on the case gaskets so your water resistance rating stays as reliable in year eight as it was on day one.
